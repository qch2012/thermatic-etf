Method

How a fund gets on this page, how it is measured, and how it is scored. Each rule is followed by the reason it exists.

Which funds are screened

  • Start with every ETF in the Nasdaq directory, leaving out test issues.

    Why: the directory lists every ETF that trades in the US, so no fund is missed just because nobody thought of it.

  • Drop funds whose names show they are leveraged or inverse, option-income, buffer, bond, spot crypto or futures funds, and a short list of named tickers.

    Why: these funds do not simply hold a basket of companies in a theme. Their results come from leverage, options or other assets, so comparing them with thematic stock funds would mislead.

  • Sort each remaining fund into a theme by its name. The rules run in order and the first match wins. A per-ticker override beats the rules.

    Why: the name is the one description every fund has. Taking the first match means a name with two theme words lands in one theme, in a predictable way. Overrides fix the few funds whose names do not say what they hold.

  • Each theme sits in one of four groups: Technology, Physical world, Social or Broad thematic.

    Why: it follows Morningstar's grouping, which keeps a long list of themes browsable in four chunks.

How age is measured

The size gate and the performance window both use a fund's age.

  • Age is the days between the first trade date and the last price date, divided by 365.25.

    Why: 365.25 averages in leap years, so a fund's age in years does not drift.

Gates: a fund must pass all four, in this order

A fund that fails stays in the data. Its status shows the first gate it failed.

  • Price data: the fund must have a price history. Status if not: "No price data".

    Why: without prices we cannot work out its age, how much it trades or how it performed.

  • Net assets: a net-asset figure must exist from some source. Status if not: "No net-asset data". A figure from a fallback source is marked *.

    Why: size is 40% of the score. A guessed number would put an invented value into the ranking, so a missing one is shown as missing.

  • Size: net assets of at least $100M for a fund aged 3 years or more, and at least $25M for a younger fund. Status if not: "Under $100M (established minimum)" or "Under $25M (emerging minimum)".

    Why: very small funds are more likely to close and are harder to trade. Younger funds get a lower bar because every fund starts small.

  • Liquidity: the average of close times volume over the last 63 trading days must be at least $0.5M a day. Status if not: "Under $0.5M avg daily $ volume".

    Why: a fund that barely trades is hard to buy or sell at a fair price. 63 trading days is about three months, long enough to smooth out one quiet week.

The performance window

Each theme has one window, shared by all of its funds.

  • Measured funds are the eligible funds aged 0.5 years or more. Younger funds are flagged 🆕 and do not affect the window.

    Why: a few weeks of prices say little about performance, and one brand-new fund should not shorten the window for everyone else.

  • The window ends on the latest trading date that all measured funds share. It starts at the later of two dates: the latest launch date among measured funds, or the end minus 3 years. The start is then moved forward to the first shared trading date on or after it.

    Why: every fund is judged over exactly the same dates, so none can look better by being measured over a kinder stretch. The 3-year cap keeps old market episodes from dominating.

  • Only dates that every measured fund traded on are used, and no prices from before the start are used.

    Why: returns and volatility are then computed on identical days for every fund, so they can be compared directly.

  • The window length L is the calendar days from start to end, divided by 365.25. If L is under 0.5 years, or the theme has no measured funds, performance is unmeasured for the whole theme.

    Why: under six months of data is mostly noise. Showing "unmeasured" is more honest than ranking on it.

The numbers shown

  • Return is the cumulative total return: end price divided by start price, minus 1, using adjusted close.

    Why: adjusted close includes distributions, so a fund that pays out income is not marked down for it.

  • Annualized return is (end price divided by start price) raised to 1/L, minus 1. This applies to every measured window, including those under a year.

    Why: it puts windows of different lengths on a per-year scale, so they can be compared.

  • Volatility is the sample standard deviation (n minus 1) of daily log returns on the shared dates, times the square root of 252.

    Why: it measures how bumpy the ride was. 252 is the usual number of trading days in a year, so the figure is per year.

  • Ratio is annualized return divided by annualized volatility. Higher is better. It is not the Sharpe ratio: no risk-free rate is subtracted.

    Why: it rewards return per unit of bumpiness. Leaving out a risk-free rate means it depends only on the funds' own prices, so no outside number is needed.

The score: 0 to 100, within one theme only

  • Each part of the score is a percentile among the eligible funds in the same theme: rank divided by the number of funds, with tied funds sharing the average rank. The best fund gets 1.0. A sole fund gets 1.0.

    Why: percentiles put size, fees and return-per-risk on one scale, and one extreme fund cannot swamp the rest.

  • Size counts 40% (net assets, higher is better). Cost counts 20% (expense ratio, lower is better). Performance counts 40% (the ratio, with the percentile taken among measured funds only).

    Why: size shows a fund is likely to survive and be easy to trade, and performance is the point of picking a fund, so both count most. Cost counts less but never stops: it is paid every year. The sliders above let you try other weights.

  • A missing expense ratio, or unmeasured performance, gets a neutral 0.5. Unmeasured performance is flagged †.

    Why: a fund is neither rewarded nor punished for something we cannot see.

  • Score = 100 × (0.4 × size + 0.2 × cost + 0.4 × performance). Funds are ranked by score. Ties go to the larger fund.

    Why: when scores are equal, the fund with more assets is the safer pick.

  • Scores are not comparable across themes.

    Why: a score only says how a fund ranks among its own theme's eligible funds. A 70 in one theme and a 70 in another mean different things.

  • Average daily dollar volume is shown but not scored.

    Why: it rises with size, so scoring it would count size twice.

What the page shows

  • Top 3 per theme, with no minimum score.

    Why: a short list is easier to act on. The gates already filter out weak funds, so a score floor would only hide the best fund in a weak theme.

  • Themes are grouped, and ordered by the net assets of their largest fund.

    Why: the themes with the most money in them come first.

  • Themes with no eligible fund are listed separately, with their candidates and the reason each failed.

    Why: a theme that is missing from the ranking should read as "nothing passed the gates", not "we forgot it".

  • The sliders change the weights and the top N. Gates, windows and percentiles stay as the daily run computed them.

    Why: how much to value size, cost and performance is a matter of taste. Gates and windows are facts about the data, and changing them would need the full price history in your browser.
